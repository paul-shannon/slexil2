Learning from Harry Moses

My Motivation
I was born in 1952, a week before Harry Moses was recorded telling the daylight story way up the Skagit River, probably near Marblemount. I was born abroad, my father serving in the U.S. Army in Europe after WWII. I grew up in the post-war boom, in a stern hierarchical nuclear family, absorbing world-views and sensibilities which, by the time I turned seven, I could tell did not serve me well. Competition, isolated individualism, relentless growth, hard-nosed profiteering: these principles ruled the world.

In my seventh year, by accident, on a random bike ride, I found my way to a Kansas forest. I remember still the beauty that day: sun glinting on a river moving slowly over flat round rocks, trees on either side and as far as I could see, a bright blue sky. That day set me on my course. Now 65 years later, after a career in radio astronomy, biology and medicine - those all in pursuit of the beauty of the natural world.

And that day also leads me here. When my own son, who is now 27, when he was young, we read lots of books together. Often animal stories. And then increasingly Pacific Northwest animal stories. At age 5, I asked him, "Leo, do you want to learn Lushootseed?". He said "sure". We found a couple of teachers in Vi Hilbert's orbit, including Zeke. Leo interests shifted to little league baseball, so we did lots of that.

By this time I had found Vi and Thom's 1976 scholarly work on the Harry Moses story. I was immediately hooked. My sciencey predilections had long before led to a study of photosynthesis - the ancient and rather complex biochemistry by which sunlight is captured - which, not incidentally, underlies and creates all of our life on this planet. All in a virtuous way: no-one is hurt by this bit of biochemistry, no-one is deprived of anything, pretty much all of the good things we know depend upon, and exist downstream of photosynthesis.

Along the way, charmed by the story, and hungering still for some new ways to understand the world, I encountered the Lushootseed language itself. Which, from the modest amount I have learned, fits these descriptions:

From Wittgenstein: A whole cloud of philosophy condensed into a drop of grammar.

From early 20th century anthropologist/linguist Edward Sapir: Single Algonquin words are like tiny imagist poems.

Polysynthetic & agglutinative: Yes, Lushootseed is considered a polysynthetic language, a defining characteristic of many Salishan languages. It utilizes a high number of morphemes—affixes, reduplication, and lexical suffixes—to combine complex ideas, such as time, aspect, and mood, into single word structures that can resemble whole sentences in other languages.

Key aspects of Lushootseed's polysynthetic structure include:

Rich Morphology: It features extensive derivational and inflectional affixes, with words constructed from simple roots paired with multiple morphemes.

Lexical Suffixes: A distinctive feature of Salishan languages is the use of 100–150 specialized lexical suffixes that add nominal or conceptual information directly to verbs.

Incorporation: The language frequently incorporates elements, allowing a single word to carry significant grammatical and semantic information.

Reduced Noun/Verb Distinction: Like many in its family, Lushootseed often blurs the distinction between noun and verb, with most roots capable of taking inflection, such as aspect markers typically found on verbs.

While described as polysynthetic, it is sometimes characterized alongside its neighbors as having extreme, yet stable, morphological complexity.

Which is pretty much the story Harry Moses tells. Today I will focus mostly on Harry and his beautiful language, but the end, time permitting, and interest detected, I will spend a minute of two illustrating the parallel between Mink & Raven's actions, and the biochemistry.

One last thing. That family and culture I grew up in? It had its' implicit stories, guides to what the world is, and what is good conduct. How Daylight was Stolen teaches different stories. It has humor, cunning, wisdom, non-violent and non-destructive skill at obtaining things of value, bravery, humility, a recognition that valuable things can be sought - but be careful when you get them, because putting them to work is not always easy.

syəsyəhub
From Thom Hess: Most syəsyəbub are fairly light and humorous, reflecting the foibles of human nature. ... almost every story serves to teach appropriate behavior. This instruction is usually achieved by humourously detailing the unfortuante, even disatrous, consequnces of breaking taboos.

Some are more serious, longer, and concerned with graver questions. They examine the world order and deal with points of tension within the social fabric. Their major characters are heroes who act wisely rather than impetuously or foolishly as in the lighter stories.

However, the dichotomy into lighter, shorter tales and longer, graver ones is only a tendency. Elements characteristic of one type of syəsyəbub are frequently found in the other; the Lushootseed people themselves do not judge the difference sufficient to warrant separate Lushootseed terms for the two types.

The events recounted in syəsyəhub took place before the word was transformed. Long ago, before the change, there were no humans as we know them. All of the beings of this age shimmered among humanoid, animal, and spirit forms; but they always had the same emotions and sensibilities as humans. When people did arrive, the world had been prepared for them by the transformer, who had arranged for all of the beings to take on the fixed forms of animals, plants, and geological features.

During the Myth Age, beings had some of the characteristics of the modern species with the same names. In addition, some of the denizens of the old world order have some of the characteristics we know them for today. For example, Raven, when a man, could fly and he was an unbridled glutton, an apt parallel to the scavenging habits of the present birds.

These characters, each with human and animal traits, provide the raconteur with great flexibility. He can draw upon one or the other of these attributes as plot, imagination, and sense of humor require.

Supernatural power, a potency neutral in and of itself, is a third attribute of the early beings who preceded humans on the earth. By means of this power, they performed feats that no creature, human or otherwise, could do today.

The old order did not last however. At some time in the remote past a being of great power walked through the world changing everything into its present form, preparing the land for the Native people who were soon to come.

Three beings of the prechange era are particularly prominent in Lushootseed literature. These are Mink, Raven, and Coyote. All three are trickster, impulsive characters who are utterly incapable of controlling their appetites and passions. No deception is too immoral or dishonest for them when trying to gratify their base desires. Their attempts at deception are often farfetched and always humorous. As often as they trick others, they themselves are either tricked or caught in their duplicity. And yet these amoral fellows do, on occasion, rise to heroic deeds for the benefit of others.

For example, it was Mink and Raven who managed to procure daylight for their people.

Relative Isolation of the Upper Skagit

From Bruce Miller

At the time of the Treaty of Point Elliott in 1855, members of [the eleven predecssor] bands carried on fishing, shellfishing, hunting and gathering activities from the saltwater areas all the way to the mountainous upper reaches of the Skagit River, adjacent areas and, for hunting and gathering, even into Eastern Washington. Upper Skagit people hunted game (including deer, elk, cougar, and mountain goat), on mountains and high locations, at streams and lakes, at meadows and on the plains.

A two-mile logjam at the site of the present-day town of Mt. Vernon discouraged white settlement along the Skagit River until it was dynamited in 1878, but Upper Skagit people resisted the movement of settlers and surveyors up the Skagit Valley. Conflict in 1886 led the Upper Skagit to warn all settlers to leave the area or be harmed. More than one hundred canoes of people met with settlers to protest the seizure of their lands.

Harry Moses Anecdotes
Sally Snyder: During the years I knew non-reservation Skagits they were also surrounded by White neighbors and were even more of a minority population where they lived than Reservation Indians. But they were economically independent, with standards of living which did not distinguish them from Caucasian neighbors. They seemed to have been socially and psychologically more stable than reservation people.

Despite the physical isolation of upriver families from other Indians - so different from the community setting of reservations - there was more unity among them. Cultural decay had progressed no further, if as far, than for reservation Skagits. But it had been supplanted by Western elements which had led not to the disorganization, disturbances, and hopelessness I saw on the Reservation, but to a degree of positive identification as being both Indians and members of a predominantly White community.

biography
Harry Moses was born November 15, 1878, near Marblemount, far up the Skagit River, 50 miles from the Puget Sound. His father, Charley, was born in 1853 east of the mountains in Entiat, not far from Lake Chelan.

June Collins, in "Valley of the Spirits: the Upper Skagit Indians of Western Washington" says guite a bit about Harry Moses, including the Entiat (əntiatq̓) birth of his father, and that Harry made three winter snowshoe trips across the mountains, a distance of 112 miles. Visits and intermarriage were not uncommon. The Upper Skagit they kept a canoe hidden on Lake Chelan.

According to Collins' accounts, Harry Moses (Henry) spent significant time as a young boy working alongside Squires on the Skagit River. These formative years were centered around traditional subsistence practices, most notably the construction and maintenance of fish weirs—complex wooden traps used to harvest salmon

Fish weirs were elaborate structures built along the banks of the Skagit and its tributaries. Comprised of a vertical wooden fence and supports to hold it in place, the purpose of a weir was to keep the salmon from moving upriver in all but one part of the fence, so that fishers perched on a platform above the weir could easily dip their nets into the water and catch fish. The main channel of the Skagit was too strong and wide for weirs, but tributaries and narrow side channels made ideal weir sites. The structures were built to operate in the summer months and then be taken down or washed away by spring flooding and rebuilt the following summer (Collins 1974:48). In these interviews, Harry explained that his biological father, Charley Moses, and his mother, Mary, separated while he was young. He subsequently lived with his mother and his stepfather, John Squires, in the Marblemount area.
